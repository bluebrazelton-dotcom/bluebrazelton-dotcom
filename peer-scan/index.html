1How to read this

This scan maps how 11 educational-leadership programs collect graduate data and tell their story in public — to sharpen NAU's storytelling, design our own alumni-outcomes data collection, and benchmark where we stand.

Scope is the educational leadership department or program at each institution, not the whole college, read from public program pages as of September 2026. Alumni job success and placement is the priority lens throughout.

Sections run in order: the peer set and method, the NAU baseline we measure against, an at-a-glance comparison matrix, then depth on placement data, storytelling patterns, and data-collection methods — closing with recommended moves for NAU and a reserve pool for extending the set.

3The NAU baseline

NAU's public story rests on one memorable placement stat and a few testimonials, while its data-collection methods are not visible publicly and its outcomes storytelling is thin and split across pages.

The standout asset is a placement claim on the College of Education program page: "65% of seated Arizona superintendents are NAU graduates," with roughly 300 new leaders graduating annually. Alumni testimonials appear but are sparse — for example, Dr. Megan Garvy of Mesa Community College, on how the program "refined my academic writing" and enabled "large-scale transformational projects."

By contrast, the department's own site is program-descriptive: no placement statistics, no alumni or current-student spotlights, and faculty reachable only through a directory link. No public evidence shows how the department collects graduate outcome data — no alumni survey, first-destination tracking, or outcomes dashboard is surfaced (internal practice may differ).

The structure peers should be matched against: an EdD in K-12 Administration and an EdD in Community College / Higher Education Administration, four master's tracks, and distinctive American Indian School Leadership and International Educational Leadership emphases.

Net: the 65% superintendent stat is a strong, quotable bar — but it stands almost alone, under-supported by systematic data and under-told across the department's own channels.

5Alumni job success & placement (priority)

Public placement data at the true program level is rare — only Western Michigan reports it cleanly for its ed-leadership department; everywhere else the useful numbers sit at the college or accreditation level, or do not exist. A credible department-scale placement snapshot is therefore the clearest whitespace NAU could own. The reporting models we found, strongest to weakest:

Department-level outcomes with a real method (the model to beat). Western Michigan's ELRT department publishes a 2024-25 Career Outcomes Report: 94% of graduates working full time have a job related to their degree, and its Higher Education Leadership PhD claims 100% employed or continuing education within three months. It is backed by a transparent NACE first-destination method — a survey sent about three weeks before graduation, four reminders, then LinkedIn supplementation, reaching a 78% knowledge rate.

NACE-grade career reporting at the college level. Vanderbilt Peabody reports 95% of 2024-25 graduates placed by November 15 via a NACE-compliant annual report with a 98% knowledge rate and named employers, and its LPO department keeps a simple Recent PhD Placements list of name, title, and employer — though it has gone stale since 2022.

Accreditation-driven outcomes (CAEP). East Carolina and Old Dominion surface their most defensible numbers through educator-prep accreditation: ECU's CAEP Annual Report includes an employment-outcomes measure, and ODU's CAEP page breaks out Educational Leadership with an employer-satisfaction mean of 4.22/5 and a 100% licensure-exam pass rate. Both are college/EPP-level, not EdD-specific.

Named-leader outcomes (story as proof). Several programs substitute concrete named placements for rates: ECU names Matthew Cheeseman, EdD '22, now a county superintendent; U of A features Dr. Flori Centeno Huitt, EdD '23, Assistant Superintendent at Tucson Unified; and Sam Houston's strongest alum, Dr. LaTonya Goffney, 2018 Texas Superintendent of the Year, appears only in a university alumni-relations page, not the department's.

College-level stat tiles. ASU leans on a college-wide 90% employed in their field within six months; Michigan State stays qualitative, citing "high rates of promotion and long-term career success" backed by rankings rather than numbers.

Completion transparency instead of placement. GCU publishes no placement data, with an explicit disclaimer, but posts an unusually candid doctoral outcomes table: 1,226 EdD in Organizational Leadership graduates, a 6.5-year average completion, and cost — completion and time-to-degree, not jobs.

The pattern is clear: a department that publishes even a modest, methodologically honest placement snapshot stands out immediately, because almost no ed-leadership program does.

6Storytelling patterns

Every program tells stories, but the ones that land treat it as a repeatable system — a recurring feed, series, or template — rather than a handful of static pages, and the best tie each name to a real job title.

Alumni storytelling clusters into five formats. The strongest is a content engine: East Carolina's LEED news blog continuously rotates alumni, student, and faculty spotlights, such as Carrie Morris, EdD. Next is a cadenced series — UNLV runs a numbered, dated Alumni Spotlight. Michigan State uses self-reported Alumni Notes in its alumni magazine, organized by name and degree; GCU runs a high-volume, video-first testimonial engine. Western Michigan and Vanderbilt use the simplest high-trust format — named alumni with degree year and current role, from a community-college president to a CEO.

Current-student storytelling is thinner everywhere, but two models stand out. Old Dominion's student-profile pages give about a dozen photo-plus-interview profiles, each naming the student's employer, role, faculty mentor, and research focus — which doubles as informal placement proof. Vanderbilt's LLO feature names current doctoral students alongside their day jobs. Most others — ASU, U of A, Ball State, Sam Houston, Michigan State — have little or none.

Faculty storytelling takes three recurring shapes. Ball State's faculty directory pairs headshots with CVs and individual bio pages; ASU publishes monthly faculty scholarship and recognition roundups; UNLV runs a research-and-experts news feed (a $1M NSF grant, faculty in national media). GCU is the weak outlier, presenting faculty collectively with no named profiles.

The recurring failure is good material stranded in the wrong place: Sam Houston's Texas Superintendent of the Year and Michigan State's rich alumni notes live in university or college channels, and U of A's dedicated spotlight page returns a 404 — so the story never reaches the program page where a prospective student actually looks.

7Data-collection methods & infrastructure

How programs gather outcome data falls into four approaches, most to least rigorous: NACE first-destination surveys, accreditation reporting, self-reported alumni submissions, and aggregated institutional records. Most programs run nothing visible; the ones that stand out have a defined instrument.

NACE first-destination surveys (the gold standard). Western Michigan's documented method is the most copyable: survey graduates about three weeks before they finish, send four reminders, then fill gaps from public LinkedIn profiles, and report the knowledge rate (78%). Vanderbilt Peabody runs the same NACE-compliant approach at college scale (98% knowledge rate), and East Carolina runs a university First-Destination Survey through its IR office.

Accreditation reporting (data you may already generate). For the K-12 and principal side, CAEP educator-prep accreditation requires completer, employer-satisfaction, and employment-outcome data. East Carolina's CAEP Annual Report, Old Dominion's CAEP measures, and Ball State all publish it. This matters for NAU: the K-12 administration programs likely already produce accreditation data that is not surfaced publicly.

Self-reported alumni submissions (low cost, lower rigor). Michigan State collects updates through an Alumni Notes submission and a Qualtrics awards form; ASU runs an alumni story-submission survey plus a CareerLink platform; GCU relies on self-recorded video testimonials. These build a story pipeline cheaply but do not produce defensible rates.

Aggregated institutional records. GCU's completion and cost table is built from its student-information system, while Ball State and Old Dominion rely on university-wide alumni surveys from their IR offices — though ODU's sit behind a login. These are broad but rarely broken out by department.

A shared vocabulary: CPED. ASU, U of A, and East Carolina anchor their EdDs in the Carnegie Project on the Education Doctorate (dissertation-in-practice, problems of practice), giving a common language for defining program outcomes — ASU's EdD was named CPED "Program of the Year." NAU could use the same framing to make its outcomes legible to peers.

8Takeaways & recommended moves for NAU

NAU already owns the single strongest public claim in this peer set — 65% of Arizona's sitting superintendents — but it is unmethodical and stranded on a College page. The opportunity is not better raw material; it is systematic collection, department-page presence, and a storytelling cadence. Moves are grouped by our three uses, hardest-hitting first.

Build our own data collection.

  1. Run a NACE-style first-destination survey (copy Western Michigan): send it about three weeks before graduation, four reminders, then supplement from LinkedIn, and report a knowledge rate. Smallest first step — do it for one EdD cohort this year.
  2. Surface the accreditation data we likely already collect. The K-12 side almost certainly produces CAEP-style completer, employer-satisfaction, and employment figures (as ECU and ODU publish) — pull those numbers and post them.
  3. Keep a simple "Recent Graduate Placements" list — name, title, employer — refreshed each cohort (copy Vanderbilt's PhD placements list); trivial to start, high credibility.

Sharpen our storytelling.

  1. Move the 65% superintendent stat — plus any new placement numbers — onto the department's own page as headline stat tiles (copy ASU and WMU), not just the College page.
  2. Start a cadenced spotlight series: one alumni, student, or faculty profile a month, each naming a real role (copy UNLV's numbered series or ECU's rotating blog).
  3. Add current-student profiles that name employer, role, and research focus (copy ODU) — which doubles as informal placement proof.
  4. Open a low-cost alumni "share your story" submission form (copy MSU's Alumni Notes) to keep the pipeline fed without heavy staff time.

Benchmark where we stand. NAU's headline claim beats nearly every peer's public data; the gap is method, department-page presence, and cadence — not evidence. Before investing, consider re-running this scan against the 12 reserve ABOR peers to confirm the whitespace is real.

If we do one thing: run a first-destination survey for the next graduating cohort and post the result — next to the 65% stat and a short placements list — on the department's own page. That alone would move NAU past most of this peer set.

To extend defensibly, in order:

  1. Prioritize reserve peers whose doctoral programs mirror NAU's dual K-12 + higher-ed structure.
  2. Confirm public outcomes or storytelling pages exist before committing a unit — skip programs with nothing public to compare.
  3. Add an aspirational exemplar only to study a specific practice worth copying, not for coverage.
  4. Re-run the same rubric — placement data, storytelling by audience, collection method — so new rows stay comparable.
